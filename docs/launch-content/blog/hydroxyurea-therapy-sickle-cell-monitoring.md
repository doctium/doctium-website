# Hydroxyurea Therapy for Sickle Cell: Why Dosing Is Individual

Hydroxyurea therapy for sickle cell disease is one of the few treatments that reliably reduces painful crises, acute chest syndrome, and transfusion need. But the dose that helps one patient is rarely the dose that helps the next. Two people with the same genotype, the same weight, and the same starting prescription can land in very different places once their marrow responds. That is why hydroxyurea is best understood as an n-of-1 treatment: the right dose is discovered for each patient through careful dose-response tracking, not assigned from a table and left alone.

In Nigeria, where sickle cell disease is concentrated more heavily than anywhere else in the world, this matters at scale. Clinics carry large haematology caseloads, and titration decisions repeat across hundreds of patients. The clinical challenge is consistency: tracking each individual's response, watching the bloodwork, and adjusting safely without losing the thread between visits.

## Why hydroxyurea dosing has to be personalised

Hydroxyurea works by raising fetal haemoglobin and reducing the sickling that drives crises. The therapeutic effect tends to track with reaching a patient's maximum tolerated dose, but "tolerated" is the operative word. Push too hard and you suppress the marrow; stay too low and the patient keeps having crises while taking a drug that is not yet doing its job.

The variables are individual. Marrow reserve, adherence, baseline counts, body weight changes, intercurrent infection, and genotype all shift the picture. A dose that is comfortable in one patient causes neutropenia in another. There is no shortcut around this. Effective hydroxyurea titration means starting at a sensible dose, watching the response, and stepping up or holding based on what the blood actually shows.

## The role of CBC monitoring and safety flags

CBC monitoring is the safety backbone of hydroxyurea therapy. Because the drug suppresses the bone marrow as part of how it works, clinicians watch the complete blood count to stay inside a safe window. Falling neutrophils, dropping platelets, or a haemoglobin moving the wrong way are the signals that a dose has gone too far and needs to be held or reduced.

The difficulty is rarely knowing the rule. It is applying it reliably across a busy clinic, visit after visit, patient after patient. A result that needs action can sit in a stack of normal ones. This is where structured tracking earns its place: every count tied to the patient's dose history, with the concerning values surfaced rather than buried.
