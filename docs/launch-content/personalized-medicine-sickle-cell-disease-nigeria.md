# Personalized Medicine for Sickle Cell Disease in Nigeria: From Genotype to n-of-1 Care

Few places on earth make the argument for personalized medicine as clearly as sickle cell care in Nigeria. The country is the global epicenter of sickle cell disease, genotype testing is cheap and culturally normalized, and the single most important disease-modifying therapy, hydroxyurea, is genuinely an n-of-1 problem: the right dose for one patient is the wrong dose for another, and the only way to find it is careful, repeated titration against that person's own blood counts and crisis history.

That combination is rare. In most settings, personalized medicine waits on expensive sequencing or biomarkers that few patients can access. Here, the raw ingredients already exist in everyday clinics. What has been missing is the connective tissue: a system that holds a patient's genotype, weather, crisis pattern, lab trends and medication history together, and turns them into clear, explainable guidance for the clinician who is still making every call.

## Why Nigeria is the right place to start

Personalized medicine for sickle cell does not require inventing new science. It requires organizing what is already known about a specific patient and presenting it at the moment a decision is made.

Three local realities make the case:

- **Scale and concentration.** Sickle cell disease is common enough in Nigeria that clinicians manage it as routine chronic care, not as a rare exception. That density is exactly what makes pattern-aware tooling worth building.
- **Genotype is accessible.** SS, SC and AS status is widely tested and widely understood by patients and families. Care can be genotype-aware from the first encounter rather than waiting on a workup most people will never get.
- **Hydroxyurea is n-of-1 by nature.** Response varies. Dosing is adjusted up or down against CBC results and tolerance over months. This is the textbook case for individualized, monitored therapy rather than a fixed protocol.

Add environmental triggers like the harmattan, when dry, dusty, cold air is associated with more crises, and you have a disease where personalization is not a luxury. It is how good care already works. Software's job is to make that easier to do well and harder to do badly.

### Genotype-aware care thresholds

The system adapts its thresholds and prompts to genotype (SS, SC, AS) rather than treating every patient identically. An expected baseline for one phenotype is a warning sign for another. Genotype-aware thresholds mean alerts and reference ranges reflect the patient in front of you, not a population average that may not apply.

### An explainable risk engine with named factors

Risk scores are only useful if a clinician can interrogate them. Doctium's risk engine is explainable by design: every score breaks down into named, weighted factors the doctor can read and challenge. Those factors include the patient's own history and live local context such as weather and harmattan conditions. Instead of an opaque number, the clinician sees what is driving it and how much each input contributes, which keeps accountability where it belongs.

### A crisis diary that builds the longitudinal picture

Vaso-occlusive crises are the lived reality of the disease, and their pattern matters. A structured crisis diary lets patients and clinicians record events over time, building the longitudinal record that makes titration decisions and risk assessment meaningful rather than guesswork from the last visit alone.

### Hydroxyurea titration with CBC safety flags

This is where n-of-1 care becomes concrete. Doctium supports hydroxyurea titration by surfacing CBC trends and raising safety flags, including hold-dose guidance routed to the doctor when counts warrant caution. The guidance goes to the clinician. Patients never self-dose, and the system does not prescribe autonomously. The doctor reviews the data, the flag and the recommendation, then decides.

### A daily monitoring agent and a pre-visit brief

Between appointments, a daily monitoring and care agent helps patients stay engaged with logging and check-ins. Ahead of each consultation, the clinician receives a pre-visit brief that summarizes what changed, what to watch and what the data suggests, so limited consultation time is spent on decisions rather than reconstruction. Anonymized data also feeds administrative outcomes tracking and research, contributing to a better evidence base for sickle cell care over time.

## The safety stance: rules outrank the model

Personalization is powerful, which is exactly why its limits have to be firm. Doctium's approach to sickle cell care holds to a few non-negotiable principles:

- **Rules outrank the model.** Deterministic clinical rules sit above the AI. When a hard safety rule and a model suggestion conflict, the rule wins.
- **Safety rails are never personalized.** Thresholds and care can adapt to the individual; the rails that prevent harm do not bend to convenience or preference.
- **Doctor-in-the-loop, always.** AI summarizes, scores, monitors and recommends. A licensed clinician remains responsible for every clinical decision.
- **Patients never self-dose.** Titration guidance, including any hold-dose flag, is directed to the doctor, not the patient.

This is what makes individualized therapy responsible rather than reckless. The system narrows the search space and shows its reasoning; the clinician retains control.
